DoseCam light mechanism, camera gain and film uncertainty

Research date: 2026-11-16. Local analysis only; not deployed. This note supersedes the unsupported mechanism and uncertainty claims in the original handoff. Calculations: scripts/light_budget.py; numeric output: light_budget.json.

Scope update, 2026-11-16: Eric does not plan scintillation-versus-Cherenkov separation measurements. The presentation simply notes that primary Cherenkov light should be internally reflected in the current geometry. The component fractions below are retained as exploratory sensitivity calculations, not a measurement program or a validated decomposition. The primary-cone TIR result does not depend on camera viewing angle; camera-angle examples below are retained only as part of the exploratory collection model. All film wells are treated on the same basis, with no row-dependent correction. The aluminum-tape target is likely nonuniform, so spot motion can change the collected light; this contribution has not been quantified.

Findings and confidence

Scintillation is a credible, potentially dominant source of the recorded spot. The statement “mostly Cherenkov” is unsupported. Undoped PC scintillates; doping is not required. The user's geometry also strongly suppresses the direct, primary-electron Cherenkov contribution into the camera. A unique experimental percentage cannot be extracted from the four broadband frames.

A useful conditional benchmark is about 70% scintillation / 30% Cherenkov after sensor QE weighting, if both mechanisms have equal optical throughput. That is a sensitivity-model result, not a measured split or a confidence interval. It uses a weakly established absolute scintillation-yield scale. In a single-bounce rough-reflector model appropriate to the stated geometry, a worked example gives about 73% scintillation / 27% Cherenkov, assuming 20% relative UV throughput and 10% diffuse reflection. Those two inputs are unmeasured: the example shows sensitivity to assumed optics, not a measured 73% fraction. No component-separation measurement is planned.

The defensible present conclusion is scintillation probably contributes substantially; scintillation dominance is the working hypothesis for a mostly smooth target. The evidence does not establish a lower bound on its camera fraction. Strong UV attenuation, foil facets, adhesive fluorescence, and secondary-electron light can change the result. No measured uncertainty distribution exists for these effects.

Camera noise accounting: the four archived peak fits have 0.35–0.39% nominal 1σ errors from residual-scaled covariance. This is one combined contribution: photon and read/background noise are not added again. Gain-dependent component projections are diagnostics; see the reproducible audit. Use 10.7 e-/DN only as a manufacturer-camera gain proxy, with 8–12 e-/DN as a sensitivity interval, not a calibrated uncertainty.

Film: use the supplied 1.9–2.4% uncertainties as standard uncertainties (1σ, k = 1) for this comparison, per E. Martin on 2026-11-17. No separate film peak-fit uncertainty is calculated here. The mixed literature on rate response remains a minor caveat; no rate-related bias is established for this run.

1. Evidence for intrinsic PC scintillation

Nakamura et al. (2014), Jpn. J. Health Phys. 49, 98–101, doi:10.5453/jhps.49.98 tested undoped Teijin PC with conversion electrons. Their relative light output was 0.67 of undoped PET; they describe PET as approximately one-seventh of a typical organic scintillator. Their optically excited spectrum peaks near 350 nm. This establishes the mechanism but supplies neither an absolute photon yield for our sheet nor a spectrum measured during our beam pulse. The source used a 5 mm plate and PMT coupling, unlike our 0.5 mm sheet and remote camera.

The reported ratio is a relative detector light output, not an independently calibrated intrinsic photons/MeV measurement. Wavelength-dependent collection, PMT sensitivity and Cherenkov contamination in the electron-source measurement limit transfer to our apparatus. Treat it as an order-of-magnitude anchor.

PDG (2024), Particle Detectors at Accelerators, sections 35.3 and 35.5 describes aromatic-matrix scintillation and wavelength-shifting dopants, gives the typical organic scintillator scale of one photon per 100 eV, and states the Cherenkov yield including collection and detector efficiency separately.

2. Photon-production estimate

Inputs from the experiment: 30 MeV electrons, 0.5 mm PC. Use n = 1.585 as a constant-index approximation. Real dispersion increases n toward the UV, so this approximation is sufficient for scale estimates, not precision optics. Beta = sqrt(1 - [1 + 30/0.511]^-2) = 0.99986.

Frank–Tamm, with wavelength and path length expressed in consistent units:

N_C = 2 pi alpha L (1 - 1/(beta n)^2) (1/lambda_min - 1/lambda_max)

The computed yield per traversing primary electron is 14.8 photons in 400–700 nm, 26.3 in 300–700 nm, or 33.4 in 300–1100 nm. Thus the old “15 visible photons” scale is reasonable only with its 400–700 nm band stated. It does not establish which component reaches the camera. Cherenkov photons below 300 nm are excluded here; absorption and possible wavelength conversion would require an additional transport model.

For scintillation, take the relative-output chain as a proxy:

Y_S,proxy = (0.67/7) * 10,000 = approximately 960 photons/MeV
DeltaE_proxy = (1.20 g/cm3) * (0.05 cm) * (2.0 MeV cm2/g) = 0.12 MeV
N_S,proxy = approximately 115 photons/electron

PDG's PC material table supplies density 1.20 g/cm3. The 2.0 MeV cm2/g collision stopping power is a rounded scaling assumption, not a retrieved ESTAR value at 30 MeV. NIST's definition distinguishes excitation/ionization loss from bremsstrahlung; the 30 MeV beam energy must not be substituted for local energy deposition in a thin sheet. Escaping delta rays also make collision loss an imperfect local deposition estimator. See NIST ESTAR and NIST definitions.

These assumptions give a production proxy of 77% scintillation / 23% Cherenkov over 300–1100 nm. Allowing a factor of two either way in the scintillation normalization moves the scintillation fraction to 63–87%. That factor of two is a stress test, not a measured uncertainty or assurance that the true yield lies inside it. An exact 80/20 claim is not justified.

3. QE, lens and geometry: production is not camera signal

Teledyne FLIR's IMX296 monochrome EMVA page includes the spectral-response curve preserved in the slide assets. Approximate readings are QE 20% at 350 nm, 43% at 400 nm and 60–65% at 450–550 nm; its tabulated 525 nm QE is 64.28%. The curve belongs to a complete Firefly camera and is a proxy for our module's response, not its factory calibration.

Our hardware log identifies an InnoMaker mono IMX296 and records a detected 10-bit MONO mode. The handoff's description as a Raspberry Pi-branded GS camera was imprecise. Color Bayer-channel QE values must not be used for this module.

The supplied curve and Fig. 4 of the PC paper were sampled approximately by eye. All points are preserved in the calculation script and JSON. Integrating the PC emission proxy and the 1/lambda^2 Cherenkov spectrum against QE, over 300–1100 nm, gives mean QE approximately 0.254 for scintillation and 0.365 for Cherenkov. Their yield-weighted signals are approximately 29 and 12, before solid angle, transmission or reflection losses. They are not detected electron counts in our camera. With equal throughput, their ratio implies 70.5% scintillation. Halving/doubling the yield proxy moves that benchmark to 54–83%.

User-supplied optical geometry (2026-11-16): beam normal to PC within a couple of degrees; camera about 30 degrees from sheet normal, viewing the untaped face; Arducam 8–50 mm C-mount zoom lens with C-CS adapter. If the observed face is different, revise the reflection model. The Arducam LN057 product page and C/CS lens documentation were located, but no quantitative 330–420 nm transmission curve was found. An absence of an IR filter would not establish UV transmission. Sensor QE alone cannot remove this uncertainty. PC grade, adhesive, coatings and sensor window transmission also matter.

From Snell's law and the Cherenkov relation:

theta_C = arccos(1/(beta*n)) = 50.88 degrees about the electron direction
theta_critical = arcsin(1/n) = 39.12 degrees at a PC-air boundary
theta_camera,internal = arcsin(sin(30 degrees)/n) = 18.39 degrees

At 2 degrees sheet tilt, primary-cone incidence on the free surface is still approximately 49–53 degrees: all of that cone undergoes total internal reflection in the ideal planar model. A flat, parallel aluminum reflector reverses the normal component of the ray but does not reduce its incidence angle. It cannot alone extract the primary cone into the camera. A cone ray needs roughly a 16-degree mirror-facet tilt to turn 51 degrees toward the camera's 18-degree internal ray in a favorable plane. There is no measured foil-facet distribution.

This excludes direct plus flat-specular primary-cone light, not all Cherenkov: roughness, foil wrinkles, edges, multiple reflections, secondary electrons, and scattering can contribute. Cherenkov-excited fluorescence is a further mixed mechanism. A spatially sharp imaged spot favors local light paths, but does not measure their proportions.

Independent verification of the angular claim

PDG Eq. 35.5 gives the cone half-angle. OpenStax, equations 25.15–25.17 gives Snell's law and the critical-angle condition. Substitution gives

sin(theta_air) = n sin(theta_C) = sqrt(n^2 - 1/beta^2) = 1.2296 > 1.

There is no real refracted angle for that ideal cone. Normal-incidence primary-cone trapping occurs if n^2 > 1 + 1/beta^2, approximately n > sqrt(2). A separate numerical 3D ray check swept all azimuths and beam tilts from -2 to +2 degrees: for n = 1.575, 1.585 and 1.64, the minimum reflected incidence angles were 48.58, 48.88 and 50.42 degrees, respectively, each above its critical angle. This is a calculation from cited laws, not a measurement on our target. The 18.39-degree number traces the camera's 30-degree external ray backward; it is not the outgoing refraction angle of the 51-degree cone.

A directly relevant theoretical example is Beloshitskii, Mikheev and Tulupov, Sov. Phys. JETP 58, 1010 (1983): they distinguish primary-electron Cherenkov light blocked by internal reflection from secondary-electron Cherenkov light that can escape. Their crystal calculation does not supply a numerical fraction for our amorphous PC target. The DIRC detector review also describes retention of angular information through specular reflections.

A straight long track at fixed wavelength produces radiation concentrated on a conical shell, whose intersection with a distant transverse plane is a ring. Real finite tracks have angular width and sidelobes; dispersion, scattering and secondary-electron directions broaden the distribution. Do not claim that all real emission inside the primary cone is identically zero. Interface/transition radiation is another contribution outside the bulk-cone calculation.

As a purely kinematic example, an electron directed along the normal with about 0.173 MeV kinetic energy has a Cherenkov angle of 18.4 degrees in PC and can feed a 30-degree external ray after a suitable reflection. Real delta-electron energy and direction are correlated; this example is not their transport model. The secondary-electron contribution has not been calculated. Its omission means that primary-cone trapping alone cannot establish the camera's total light split.

A transparent rough-reflector sensitivity model

For a single bounce, assume Al reflectivity R = 0.9, an isotropic scintillation source with direct and mirror-return contributions, and a fraction d of Cherenkov light redistributed as a Lambertian field at the backing. R and d are scenario inputs, not measured properties of this tape. Fresnel transmission and the solid-angle refraction factor cancel approximately for a common exit direction. The relative angular collection is then

G_C/G_S = 4 R d cos(theta_camera,internal)/(1+R) = approximately 1.80 d
f_S = 29.17 T / (29.17 T + 12.20 * 1.80 d)

T is the scintillation-spectrum-weighted throughput relative to Cherenkov after QE; it represents the *relative* remaining losses, not lens transmission at one wavelength. This effective two-number model does not simulate repeated bounces or localized specular glare. It cannot be fitted with these four images.

4. Conversion gain and photon-count noise

The Firefly mono IMX296 EMVA data report a 10-bit ADC, 10,276-electron saturation capacity, 4.69-electron temporal dark noise and 10.74 e-/ADU conversion gain. Their ratio 4.69/0.44 = 10.66 e-/DN and 10,276/10.74 = 957 useful DN are consistent with native 10-bit code units. The 16-bit transport-container wording does not justify multiplying gain by 64. Our own >>6 unpacking also produces 10-bit codes.

Raspberry Pi libcamera's IMX296 helper maps linear analogue gain 1 to register code 0 (0 dB). The kernel driver sets sensor controls and a native 10-bit output; the Pi 5 tuning file has black level 3840 in 16-bit scale, corresponding to 60 native DN. Neither source calibrates our e-/DN or proves that its ADC clip point equals full well. In particular, a tuning noise-model coefficient is not a conversion-gain measurement. These web versions are not verified as the installed Pi versions.

Use 10.7 e-/DN with an 8–12 sensitivity interval, not a statistical confidence interval. The existing background-pair method, reapplied to all 34 Alanine frames, gives 0.5078 DN per-pixel background RMS. This already includes quantization and correlated background effects; no separate quantization term is added to it. Dark frames alone do not calibrate conversion gain. The vendor gain is from another camera using IMX296, not this module.

Residual-covariance error and component audit

The fitter uses unweighted DN residuals with soft_l1 loss. Reported errors use the plain model Jacobian and C=s²*inv(J.T@J), where s²=sum(residual²)/(N_pixels-7). The amplitude standard error is sqrt(C[0,0]). Archived baseline-fit parameters and residual RMS reproduce 0.1602, 0.1664, 0.1480 and 0.1531 DN, or 0.379, 0.388, 0.349 and 0.370%, for B1/A1/A2/A3. No peak is refitted. This is a nominal independent-pixel covariance, not an exact robust/spatial-covariance treatment.

At the gain proxy, the measured spot occupies approximately 3970–4482 equivalent Gaussian pixels, with 443–459 electrons per peak pixel. Thus approximately two million electrons are localized in the spot, rather than spread uniformly over the full sensor.

The same amplitude influence vector propagates independent photon noise to 0.121–0.127% peak SE. Projection of all 34 background frames after the same 40 DN positive-hit despiking and quadratic baseline subtraction gives 0.124–0.133% from background variability, including its correlations. Their conditional quadrature diagnostic is approximately 0.18%. Gain is not calibrated and these no-beam backgrounds do not uniquely partition the signal-frame residuals, so the slide uses the combined 0.35–0.39% nominal fit error. Neither component diagnostic is added to it. Masking sensitivity also is not assumed independent for a quadrature total. The audit script and outputs preserve the calculation and assumptions.

Under the Poisson model, shot noise is too small to explain the observed scatter. Common variations in beam charge or target response remain possible. Operator delay/duration scans established full-signal plateaus, with the chosen gate margins much larger than expected jitter; gate clipping is not expected to contribute appreciably under those settings. Four frames cannot establish which systematic dominates. The Fano-factor discussion was removed at the user's request; it is not needed for this comparison.

5. EBT-XD at 40 Gy

Range, conventional uncertainty and FLASH bias

The usual manufacturer ranges, reproduced in the primary dosimetry studies, are 0.1–60 Gy dynamic and 0.4–40 Gy optimum. The Ashland protocol explains that declining slope raises high-dose uncertainty; 40 Gy is at the optimum range's upper edge, not a hard optical saturation threshold. The PDF was indexed by search but could not be downloaded during this session.

León-Marroquín et al. (2019), doi:10.1002/mp.13708 report approximately 3.1% red-channel uncertainty over 5–40 Gy and 2.4% green over 8–50 Gy when transferring calibrations between beam qualities. These values are not a universal budget for our scanner or a red-versus-multichannel comparison. The often-repeated 2.3% electron result is protocol dependent; do not substitute it for absolute FLASH uncertainty.

Smyth et al. (2024), doi:10.1007/s13246-024-01430-z compared channel combinations through 30 Gy, with seven-day readout rather than 24-hour readout. Green-only and RGB averaging had check-film scale-factor SDs of about 2.5–3.0%. RGB averaging is not automatically the same as a multichannel disturbance-correction algorithm. No matched 40 Gy, 24-hour, multichannel absolute uncertainty for our protocol was established.

Del Sarto et al. (2025), doi:10.3389/fphy.2025.1474416 used 24-hour flatbed readout at about 40 Gy total. Electron pulses spanned 0.7–19.3 Gy and instantaneous rates up to 4.82 MGy/s. They reported a possible 7% +/- 3.3% dose overestimate at the high-rate extreme relative to flashDiamond, conditional on that reference. This is a possible bias, not a 1-sigma film uncertainty or a correction to apply to our data. Their ~5% working film allowance does not validate ours. At 40 Gy in a 1–4 us pulse, our instantaneous rate would be 10–40 MGy/s, an extrapolation beyond that study. Gate duration is not beam pulse duration. Our beam pulse width needs independent confirmation.

The literature uses different beam conditions and measurement methods and reports mixed evidence on rate dependence. No rate correction is assigned to this run. The comparison uses the supplied film standard uncertainties below; the historical code allowances are unchanged by this presentation update.
